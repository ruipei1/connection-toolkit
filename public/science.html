The science

How we see each other
shapes how we connect.

The empathy perception gap describes a mismatch: people can see their peers as less caring than those peers see themselves.

An overlooked barrier

Students may want friendship and support while doubting that others would welcome their outreach. Those expectations can influence whether they start a conversation, ask for help, or share something personal.

What the research found

In research with Stanford undergraduates, students who perceived peers as more empathetic reported better current and later well-being. Students also tended to underestimate peers’ self-reported empathy.

Two field experiments combined information about peers’ self-reported empathy with encouragement to take small social risks. The interventions reduced the perception gap and increased social behaviors; social networks also expanded months later.

From findings to practice

The toolkit translates these ideas into reflection, discussion, and small actions. It encourages students to test expectations rather than assume that every interaction will go well.

What these findings mean

Peer reports describe how people see themselves. They are not a guarantee of how a particular person will respond. Findings from one campus should not be presented as measurements of another.

The new modular toolkit is informed by this research. Its individual short activities have not been established as independently evaluated interventions.
