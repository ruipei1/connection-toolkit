Blog / The science

Social perceptions · 5 minute read

The kindness we
don’t always see.

We can want connection while doubting that other people would welcome it. What happens when we question that expectation?

By [Author name]Research-informed perspective

01 / NOTICE

An invitation can begin
with an assumption.

Imagine wanting to talk to a classmate before class. You might enjoy the conversation yourself, but wonder whether they would. That quiet prediction can influence whether you say hello.

The empathy perception gap describes a mismatch: people can see their peers as less caring than those peers see themselves. Care may be available, yet difficult to recognize.

What do I know about this person—and what am I predicting?

02 / LEARN

What the research found.

Research from the Stanford Communities Project examined students’ perceptions of peers, social behavior, and well-being. Students who perceived peers as more empathetic reported better current and later well-being, while students tended to underestimate peers’ self-reported empathy.

Two field experiments combined information about peers’ self-reported empathy with encouragement to take small social risks. These interventions reduced the perception gap and increased social behaviors; social networks also expanded months later.

These findings offer a promising direction. They do not mean every classmate will respond warmly, or that findings from one campus automatically describe another.

03 / TRY

Curiosity is something
you can practice.

Start by noticing a prediction. Consider what you have observed and what else might explain it. Then choose a small, respectful action that gives you a chance to learn more.

You might invite someone to coffee, express appreciation, or ask a friend for a few minutes to talk. A disappointing response can happen. Respect it, and leave room for what future interactions might teach you.

Try this: write one sentence you could use to reach out, and decide when you might say it.
